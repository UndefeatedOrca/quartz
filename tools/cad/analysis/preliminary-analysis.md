# Preliminary analysis of Contemporary Argumentation and Debate

Prepared 2026-09-16. This is a first-pass interpretation, not a coded bibliometric study or a political classification of individual authors.

## Findings in brief

The archive moves from constructing the rules and intellectual justification of value debate toward a broader examination of debate's educational, institutional and political consequences. Inclusion and structural critiques become increasingly prominent, but coexist with procedural pluralism, empirical assessment and defenses of competitive debate. Publication becomes irregular in the 2010s, then stops for six years before a strongly themed 2023 revival.

## Scope and method

The manifest contains 336 records and 331 PDF files. I reviewed the title inventory across all volumes, inspected selected opening pages/abstracts and relevant conclusions, and visually inspected scanned feminist essays from 1993 and 1997. Representative full-text checks include the 2002 value-debate forum, Solt and Shanahan's 2004 essays, Matz and Bruschke (2006), Voth and Noland (2007), Harrigan (2008), the 2013/2014 war-powers article, Barker (2015/2016), Mabrey and Richards (2017), and the 2023 editorial introduction and several article abstracts. This is not a full reading of every article.

Four expected articles remain missing or misidentified. Most historical issues lack independently reconciled contents. Counts below are **catalogued content entries**, including reviews, introductions and some collections of multiple essays. They are not counts of research articles or unique underlying arguments. The original volume-24 forum compilation and volume-38 full issue are excluded to avoid double counting. Three front/back matter records are also excluded; the 2023 welcome remains an editorial content entry. See `period-counts.json`.

## 1. Content over time

| Period | Main emphasis | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1980s | Value/non-policy propositions, presumption, criteria, topicality, justification, judging and counterwarrants | A young debate organization works out what good arguments and fair decisions should look like. The journal is predominantly directed toward practitioners. |
| 1990s | Continuing technical theory alongside feminism, participation, harassment, urban debate, critical thinking and educational legitimacy | Debate increasingly becomes an object of social and institutional criticism. The 1993 feminism exchange, 1997 feminist critique and 1998 urban-debate cluster are important markers. |
| 2000s | Critical versus policy debate, factional conflict, gaming, digital technology, diversity, election rhetoric, genocide and public argument | A broadening of subject matter and an explicit dispute over the activity's purpose. The 2002 retrospective treats value debate as an organizational history; the 2004 forum stages competing visions of debate. |
| 2010–2017 | Topic selection, classroom pedagogy, assessment, digital practice, staffing/resources, parliamentary debate and Snider's legacy | Strong attention to demonstrating educational value and sustaining programs. The thematic clusters mean year-to-year changes are often editorial choices rather than field-wide changes. |
| 2023 | Louisville Project, Black radical argument, anti-Blackness, trans argumentation, performance and institutional exclusion | A deliberately focused special issue, with structural criticism and political commitments central to its framing. |

One especially useful distinction is between **widening access to debate** and **changing what counts as legitimate debate**. Earlier participation and urban-debate work often centers access and educational opportunity. Later work increasingly examines whether apparently neutral norms of evidence, civility, argument and competition reproduce exclusion. This is a change in emphasis, not a clean chronological boundary: Crenshaw's 1993 essay already questions dominant argumentative forms.

Another trend is increasing editorial concentration. Examples include gaming in 2003, digital debate in 2006, topic selection in 2010, digital debate/assessment in 2012, British parliamentary debate in 2015/2016, the Snider memorial volume in 2017 and the Louisville Project issue in 2023. Comparisons need to account for these clusters.

## 2. Publication frequency and volume

| Period | Volumes | Catalogued content entries | Entries per volume |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1980–1989 | 1–10 | 90 | 9.0 |
| 1990–1999 | 11–20 | 89 | 8.9 |
| 2000–2009 | 21–30 | 95 | 9.5 |
| 2010–2017 | 31–37 | 46 | 6.6 |
| 2023 | 38 | 11 | 11.0 |

The early record is consistent with roughly annual volumes through 2012. Later dating breaks that pattern: volume 34 is labelled 2013/2014, volume 35 is labelled 2015/2016, and both volumes 36 and 37 are dated 2017 in their PDFs. These are publication labels, not independently established release dates. The 2023 editor's welcome explicitly confirms the six-year hiatus following 2017. It would be incorrect to invent missing annual volumes for 2018–2022.

The indexed number of items is broadly stable near nine per volume through 2009 and lower in 2010–2017. However, fewer items do not necessarily mean much less text. Volume 34 has three substantial articles covering printed pages 1–128; its war-powers article alone spans pages 51–128. The 2023 full issue has 191 numbered body pages plus front matter. PDF page counts are unsuitable as a simple output metric because scans contain two-page spreads, publisher copies contain extra cover/copyright pages, and full issues duplicate article files.

JMU currently lists volume 38 (2023) as its current volume. That supports “no later volume shown on this publisher archive,” not a definitive claim that no later issue exists anywhere.

## 3. Political orientation

**The clearest preliminary trend is toward greater prominence of critical-left scholarship, especially in the latest issue.** That is an interpretation of arguments and editorial selection, not a measurement of authors' party affiliations.

- **Early period:** Titles and theoretical disputes primarily concern argument quality, procedural fairness and educational practice. A useful intellectual description is procedural pluralism: competing views should face common standards and adjudication. This is not evidence that the journal or its authors were politically conservative, centrist or apolitical.
- **1990s:** Feminist criticism and unequal participation become visible recurring concerns. Crenshaw's 1993 essay criticizes reductive treatment of feminism; Wilkins and Hobbs's 1997 article investigates patriarchal practices and student empowerment. These are substantive critical commitments, not simply the appearance of gender-related keywords.
- **2000s:** Critical and anti-imperialist arguments are openly contested. Shanahan's 2004 essay engages settler-colonial privilege and exclusion; Solt's essay in the same volume criticizes aspects of the critical turn and proposes ways to reconnect ideological arguments to policy debate. Harrigan's 2008 defense of switch-side debate explicitly defends argumentative pluralism against dogmatism. The journal hosts a dispute rather than one settled line.
- **2010–2017:** The archive does not support a simple uninterrupted leftward march. Assessment, staffing, pedagogy and collaboration remain central. Barker's parliamentary-debate essay argues for reducing polarization through collaboration. The war-powers article evaluates arguments on both sides, so its topic alone cannot establish a left-wing position.
- **2023:** The editorial frame and multiple contributions are explicitly aligned with Black radical, anti-racist, queer/trans liberation and, in one article's own description, Afropessimist scholarship. This issue is more coherent in its critical-left orientation than the earlier mixed forums. It also contains disagreement within critical traditions and attempts to preserve argumentative clash under alternative debate practices.

The key recurring divide is often **procedural pluralism versus arguments for transforming the institutions and norms of debate**, rather than Republican versus Democrat. Defending switch-side debate is not inherently right-wing; examining racial inequality is not by itself enough to classify an article's politics. The stronger evidence comes from the article's stated commitments and conclusions.

The 2023 issue celebrates a particular movement and was guest edited. It should not be treated as an unbiased sample of the entire debate community, or as sufficient evidence of the journal's future direction. The six-year publication gap also prevents a continuous annual trend analysis. Publication slowdown and political change coincide, but this archive does not establish a causal connection between them.

## 4. Data cautions discovered during analysis

- Volume 7's existing website metadata says 1976. Its “State of CEDA, 1986” scan clearly concerns 1986; 1976 should not be used in a chronology. Public archive metadata was not edited as part of this analysis.
- Some separate JMU article PDFs have different printed pagination from the full-issue version. For example, the separate Larsen PDF says 39–78 while the full-issue sequence places the next contribution at 77. Use a declared edition when calculating page totals; do not silently merge pagination schemes.
- Four unresolved entries are included in the 1980s catalogue count. Some forum and review records contain several contributions. Exact article output needs manual disaggregation and complete contents reconciliation.
- A reliable political time series would require a codebook, stratified full-text reading, multiple independent coders and agreement checks. Useful separate dimensions include topic, method, institutional reform versus replacement, procedural pluralism, explicit political commitments and special-issue membership. No partisan percentages have been estimated here.
